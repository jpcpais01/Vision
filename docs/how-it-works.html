Vision · Polymarket BTC 5-minute UP/DOWN

How the bot decides, every five minutes

Every five minutes Polymarket opens a new bet: will Bitcoin close higher than it was when the window opened? Vision answers it twice — once with a language model, then continuously with a simulation that overrules the model using what Bitcoin actually did while it was thinking — and trades only if the answer disagrees with the order book by enough to be worth the risk.

The bet itself

Everything downstream is a statement about one number, so it is worth being precise about which.

A market opens. Bitcoin's price at that instant becomes the barrier. Five minutes later the market settles UP if Bitcoin is strictly above the barrier and DOWN if it is not.

Both outcomes trade as separate contracts that pay $1 if right and $0 if wrong. A price of 0.58 for UP is the market saying "58% likely" — buy it, and you make 42¢ per share when it wins and lose 58¢ when it doesn't. So a probability and a price are the same kind of thing here, which is what makes the whole strategy expressible in one line: buy when our probability is meaningfully higher than their price.

Why this is hard. Over five minutes Bitcoin is very close to a coin flip. Real edges are small and rare, and the order book is not naive. Most of the code below exists to stop the bot from trading — not to make it trade.

One window, start to finish

The loop runs in the browser tab, on a 250 ms heartbeat. Here is the whole thing in the order it happens.

T+0 scapture

Pin the barrier

The bot polls Polymarket every 3 seconds for the current BTC 5-minute market. When a new one appears, it looks back through its own recorded tick history for the price at the exact moment the window opened, and locks that in as the barrier.

Getting this right matters more than anything else in the system — every probability produced later is a statement about this one number. If the bot was started mid-window and has no tick from the open, it falls back to the nearest 10-second bar and labels the barrier estimated in the interface, because being a dollar or two out is a real error and hiding it would be worse than showing it.

How the market is found

Polymarket's Gamma API is filtered for open markets whose question or slug mentions Bitcoin and an up/down framing, whose open-to-close span is genuinely 210–400 seconds, sorted by soonest close. Outcome labels are mapped onto UP/DOWN from three vocabularies (Up/Down, Yes/No, Higher/Lower) and anything unrecognised is rejected, never guessed — getting that backwards would invert every trade.

A hard-coded slug would have been simpler. The slug format for this series has already changed more than once.

T+0 sdispatch

Ask the model for a probability

The full hour goes to deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731 through OpenRouter. The prices are sent as dollar offsets from the barrier, so zero means "exactly at the line" and the model never has to do the subtraction itself.

It is asked for a calibrated probability, not a direction — told explicitly that 0.50 is the correct answer when it has no view, and that it is scored by Brier, where an overconfident wrong answer costs far more than a hedged one. A model asked "up or down?" gives you a coin flip dressed as conviction.

while waitingthe key idea

Keep recording

Nothing blocks on the model. The tick feed keeps running, the bars keep filling, and the simulation keeps producing a probability from volatility alone with a neutral 50/50 prior — so the dashboard always shows a live number, and the model's actual contribution becomes visible as the jump when its answer lands.

Those few seconds are not dead time. They are information the model could not have had, and the next stage exists to use them.

on arrivaland ~1×/s after

Overrule the model with what actually happened

This is the part that makes the system more than a wrapper around a chatbot. The model's probability is not blended in as a number. It is converted into a drift — the steady push that would have produced that probability under the volatility we measured — and then that drift is applied only to the seconds that are left.

The simulation starts from the price right now, not from the barrier. The part of the window that already happened is carried in the starting point rather than being simulated again.

The conditional update
barrier distance b = ln(S₀ / Sₜ) prior → drift m = σ · Φ⁻¹(p_llm) / √T · w prior weight w = 0.8 · (0.5 + 0.5 · confidence) terminal price S_T = Sₜ · exp(m·τ + σ√τ · Z) answer P(UP) = share of paths with S_T > S₀
S₀ / Sₜ
barrier price / price now
T / τ
full window (300 s) / seconds remaining
Z
Student-t, 4 d.o.f., standardised — crypto tails are not Gaussian

The behaviour this buys is the whole point: a model that said 75% UP, on a window where Bitcoin has since fallen $150 with 30 seconds left, comes out of the updater below 5% — because the drift needed to recover the barrier in the time remaining is not plausible at the measured volatility. A pipeline that averaged the model's number with a market price would still be buying UP.

Two engines because neither is trustworthy alone: the parametric one gets the tails wrong, the bootstrap one is hostage to whatever happened to be in its sample.

thenhaircuts

Make the number pessimistic on purpose

Two deliberate reductions are applied before the probability is allowed near an order, and both always move against taking the trade.

shrink p′ = 0.5 + (p − 0.5) × (1 − 0.10) haircut p_win = p′ − (simulation standard error) edge = p_win − ask

Every stage — the model, the vol estimate, the simulation — adds error that pushes probabilities away from the centre. Shrinking is the cheapest correction available, and it only ever costs you the trades that were marginal. Subtracting the error bar means simulation noise can never manufacture an edge.

Note that the edge is measured against the ask — the price you can actually buy at — not the midpoint. Midpoint edges look about half a spread better than they are and cannot be traded.

Sizing and execution

What happens in the roughly one second between "yes" and a filled position.

How big

Position size starts from the Kelly criterion — the mathematically optimal stake if your probability is exactly right — and is then cut down repeatedly, because it never is.

kelly f* = (p_win − ask) / (1 − ask) stake $ = bankroll × f* × 0.25 then capped at min($50, 5% of bankroll, bankroll) then capped at the shares resting at the touch shares = floor(stake / ask)

The last cap matters more than it looks: the edge was assessed against the touch price, so an order big enough to eat into the next level would be filled at a price the decision never evaluated. The bankroll also compounds — limits scale with realised P&L, not with the number typed in at the start of the session.

How it is sent

The browser proposes; the server disposes. Nothing the browser sends is trusted for anything that matters, because the browser is editable and the server is not.

  1. The browser posts an intent with a limit price of max(ask, p_win − minEdge/2) — never paying past the point where the edge is gone.
  2. The server reads the kill switch from its own store, not from the request.
  3. The server re-fetches the order book, and refuses if the market has moved past the limit, if the spread has widened, or if the price has left the configured bounds.
  4. The server re-clamps the size against its own copy of the risk config, so editing the request in devtools cannot exceed a cap.
  5. It pays at most one tick through the touch — enough to cross reliably, not enough to turn a 4¢ edge into a 1¢ one.
  6. Only then does it simulate the fill, or send it.

PAPER

Real everything, except the money.

  • Real market discovery, real live order book, real Bitcoin feed, real model calls.
  • Fills are priced by walking the actual resting book, level by level.
  • Each level is charged one tick worse than shown, standing in for the race against other takers.
  • Reports an honest partial fill when the book runs out, and never invents liquidity that was not there.
  • Refuses to fill above the configured maximum price rather than pretending the deep levels were cheap.

LIVE

Real orders. Three independent locks.

  • ALLOW_LIVE_TRADING=true must be set in the server environment — it is not a switch in the interface.
  • A Polygon private key must be configured server-side. It is read in exactly one file and never reaches the browser.
  • The request must ask for LIVE and the kill switch must be off.
  • Orders are fill-and-kill: on a five-minute market a resting remainder is a liability, not an opportunity.
  • The signing stack is behind a dynamic import, so PAPER mode never loads it at all.

Settlement

At the close the bot takes the Bitcoin price at that instant, compares it to the barrier, and marks every position on that market.

win P&L = shares × (1 − entry price) loss P&L = − shares × entry price

The window is then written to the historical record whether or not it was traded — with the model's number, the simulation's number, the volatility, both order books, the decision and the reason for any rejection. The skipped windows are the more useful half of that record: they are how you tell "the thresholds are too tight" apart from "there was genuinely no edge".

One honest caveat. In PAPER mode this settlement is the P&L. In LIVE mode the on-chain oracle decides, and it can disagree with our exchange feed at the boundary. That is exactly why the Chainlink price is tracked and its gap to the exchange feed displayed throughout the window, rather than quietly ignored.

Where the data comes from

The trading loop lives in the browser and the secrets live on the server. That split is a latency decision: the window is 300 seconds, and every hop through a serverless function is time spent on a clock that does not stop.

Why interpolated history is flagged so loudly

Only Binance publishes 1-second klines, so only Binance can reconstruct a true 10-second history an hour deep. If it is unreachable, the fallback stretches 60-second candles across the 10-second grid by interpolating between closes.

The result has the right level and the right drift but understates realised volatility, because all the movement inside each minute has been smoothed away. Understated volatility makes every edge look bigger than it is. So the feed is labelled in the interface and passed to the model as unreliable rather than being silently traded on.

Why the tick history is deliberately thinned

Binance can stream fifty Bitcoin trades a second. Storing all of them would flush the buffer in under two minutes — and take the window's opening price with it, which is the one value the entire system is measured against.

So the newest price is always exact, but the retained series is thinned to about five points a second. That holds roughly twenty minutes of tape, comfortably spanning the five-minute window.

Stopping it

The kill switch sets a flag in server-side storage that is read on every single order request — so a stale browser tab left open on another machine cannot trade through it. It also cancels every resting order on the account, forces auto-trade off, and stops the engine.

Beyond that, auto-trading is off by default and stays off until switched on; every risk limit is enforced server-side and clamped into documented bounds on write; and the whole dashboard can be put behind a shared secret, since a deployment URL is public by default and in LIVE mode this page spends money.

How you know if it works

Over a few dozen five-minute bets, profit and loss is almost pure noise. These converge faster.

Brier score
mean squared error of the probability; 0.25 is a coin flip, lower is better
Brier skill
how much better than a coin flip, as a percentage
Calibration
of the windows you called 70%, did about 70% happen?
Win rate
with a Wilson interval, because 6 of 10 is not 60%
P&L, drawdown, Sharpe
the truth in the end, and noise before it

Calibration is scored over every window observed, not only the traded ones. Scoring only trades would sample exactly the windows where the model disagreed with the market — the most biased subset available. Below twenty resolved trades the dashboard says so on the page, rather than leaving you to infer it from a flattering number.

What it cannot do

  • It needs the tab open. The loop runs in the browser. Closing it stops trading — which is the safer default anyway.
  • Without durable storage configured, history is lost on every serverless cold start.
  • A barrier captured mid-window is an estimate, and a dollar of error there is a real edge error.
  • It will not trade without a forecast. No model, no position — volatility alone is not enough.
  • The edge may simply not exist. A five-minute Bitcoin binary is close to a coin flip by construction, and Polymarket's book is not naive. Run it in PAPER long enough that the calibration curve means something before risking anything real.
